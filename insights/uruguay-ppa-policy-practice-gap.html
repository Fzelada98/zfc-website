Uruguay's own tender just undercut the policy it announced

ZFC Partners — Insights · November 2026

In October, UTE's president said the utility's next wave of solar, a 500 MW target for this five-year period, would not be bought under the fixed-price, 20-year take-or-pay PPA that built most of Uruguay's grid. Weeks later, UTE opened tender P104220/2026: three 33 MW plants, 99 MW total, procured under a 20-year power purchase agreement, per the utility's own tender notice and reporting on it. The policy announcement and the actual paperwork disagree with each other by about three weeks.

That is not a rounding error in translation. It is the first real-world test of whether Uruguay's stated retreat from fixed PPAs survives contact with an actual procurement deadline, and so far it has not.

What the first tranche actually looks like

Three plants of 33 MW each, sited across Tacuarembó, Florida and the east of the country, each awardable independently. No battery storage permitted in this tranche. UTE estimates the three projects together mobilize around $75 million in private investment, call it roughly $758,000 per MW of injection capacity. Tender documents reportedly close toward the end of October, with bids opening in the last week of January 2027. All of it, start to finish, built on the exact instrument the utility's own leadership said it was moving away from.

Why the first tranche couldn't be the test case

A 33 MW solar plant is a small, standalone asset. It has no portfolio of other contracts to lean on, no scale to self-insure against a bad pricing year, and a lender underwriting it needs a revenue line that survives the full tenor of the debt. In a system running close to 99% renewable generation, with spot prices that compress toward zero in exactly the hours solar produces, merchant exposure on an asset this size is not a financeable story on a normal construction timeline. The 20-year PPA is not nostalgia. It is the only instrument that let these three projects reach a January 2027 bid deadline at all.

A policy announcement does not close a loan. A fixed-price PPA still does, which is why Uruguay just signed one anyway.

Redesigning what replaces the PPA, something indexed differently, something that shares price risk between UTE and the generator, something that prices the surplus-export reality Uruguay now lives with, takes real structuring time. That work plausibly exists somewhere inside UTE right now. It just was not ready in time for the first 99 MW, so the program reached for the tool that was.

What we think this means

Treat the "no more fixed PPAs" statement as a direction of travel, not a description of the current pipeline. The tranches that get built on a short clock, small plants, near-term bid deadlines, will likely keep landing on contracted structures that already work, whatever UTE's leadership says about the long-run plan. The genuinely different instrument, if it exists, will show up in the larger, later blocks of the 500 MW target, where there is enough scale and enough runway to design something new and still get it financed.

There is a fair counterargument. A single 99 MW tranche proves nothing about a five-year, 500 MW program, and reform inside a state utility rarely moves in a straight line from announcement to execution. That is true, and it is also exactly the point: the gap between what gets announced and what gets procured is where a developer's actual risk sits, not in the press release.

Every market in this region financing new entrants at this scale runs the same underwriting question: does the revenue line in front of a lender match the tenor of the debt behind it, regardless of what the regulator or the utility says is coming next. Our own pipeline, Olmos Wind and Frontera in Lambayeque, Aurelion and Solar II in Moquegua, totalling more than 870 MW toward a 2 GW target across LATAM by 2030, is underwritten against whatever contracted or merchant structure actually exists at financial close, not against where policy says a market is headed.

The open question

UTE still has roughly 400 MW of this five-year target left to tender after the current 99 MW. Does a later, larger tranche actually carry a different structure once there is time to design one properly, or does every tranche that reaches a real financing deadline keep reaching for the 20-year PPA, leaving "no more fixed PPAs" as rhetoric a procurement calendar never caught up to? I do not think anyone at UTE has answered that yet, including UTE.
