Colombia's wind corridor is stuck behind a consultation that already closed once

ZFC Partners — Insights · October 2026

Colombia finished consulting 235 ethnic communities along the Colectora transmission line in June 2023, clearing the way for environmental licensing. In 2026, the Ministry of Interior certified 18 more communities that must now be consulted before the line's northern segment can proceed, pushing a start date originally set for November 2022 out to April 2027, per reporting on Grupo Energía Bogotá's project. A process everyone treated as finished got reopened.

Most of the commentary on Colombian transmission reads this as a queue problem, the same genre as every other LATAM grid story: not enough lines, not enough budget, demand outrunning build-out. Colectora is not that story. The line has a developer, an award, a route and, for most of its length, concrete in the ground. What it does not have is a consultation process that stays closed once it closes.

Where the exposure actually sits

Colombia's constitutional framework, anchored in ILO Convention 169, gives indigenous and Afro-descendant communities a right to prior consultation on infrastructure crossing their territory. Colectora, a 500 kV line awarded to Grupo Energía Bogotá in February 2018, is the clearest live case of what that right does to a schedule. The southern segment, 247 kilometers from Cuestecitas to the coast, is fully built. The northern segment, Colectora to Cuestecitas, 228 kilometers through La Guajira and Cesar, is the one still moving.

Consultation with 235 communities along that corridor concluded in June 2023, five years after the award. That was the number everyone involved had underwritten as the closing input to the permitting case. In 2026 the Ministry of Interior certified 18 additional communities in the same corridor as entitled to consultation, years after the process that was supposed to have closed it.

A permitting step that can reopen after it has already closed is not a timeline risk. It is a standing liability with no maturity date.

That is the distinction a financial model tends to miss. Environmental review has a studied duration and an appeal window a lawyer can bound. Consulta previa, as practiced on this project, does not have an equivalent ceiling, because the list of communities entitled to it is not fixed at the point a developer believes the process is complete. It can be added to by an administrative act the developer does not control and cannot contest on commercial terms.

The wind pipeline waiting on someone else's project

La Guajira holds some of the best wind resource in the Americas, and a decade of project announcements has been priced against it. Colectora is the evacuation path for that resource, which makes this a different flavor of delivery risk than the congestion curtailment most Chilean and Peruvian coverage focuses on. A plant backed down for grid congestion is still connected. A plant finished in La Guajira without Colectora in service has nowhere to send its power at all.

AES Colombia, which holds operating and under-construction wind capacity in the department, has said publicly that Colectora's delays are costing it, with built or near-built capacity unable to move power out of the region on schedule. A generator in that position cannot hedge the delay, because the asset causing it belongs to another company's balance sheet, another company's consultation process, and another company's schedule. A former Minister of Mines and Energy put the broader risk plainly, describing the country as "tight" on timing given how close Colectora's current target sits to the window in which Colombia could face a supply shortfall.

What we think follows

Treat consulta previa completion as a point estimate, not a milestone. Any generator financing into a corridor with ethnic territory in its path, in La Guajira or elsewhere in Colombia, should discount the stated interconnection date of infrastructure it does not control the way it would discount a weak counterparty's credit, because functionally that is what it is: a claim on someone else's execution that an administrative act can reopen without warning.

There is a fair counterargument, and it deserves stating plainly. Consulta previa exists because infrastructure built through indigenous and Afro-Colombian territory without it has an ugly history in this region, and a mechanism that can catch a community missed the first time is a safeguard, not a flaw in the process. That is correct on its own terms. It does not change what the open-endedness does to a capital stack sized against a fixed date.

Our own pipeline sits in Peru rather than Colombia. Neither Olmos Wind, approximately 135 MW in Lambayeque with battery storage integrated into the design and Ready-to-Build targeted for the first quarter of 2028, nor Frontera, approximately 135 MW, carries this specific exposure. The principle still travels anywhere generation depends on transmission someone else is building: the interconnection date is a deliverable owned by another party, and it is worth knowing exactly whose.

The open question

Whether Colombia moves to put a hard close date on consulta previa certification, the way some of the region's environmental-licensing regimes have added statutory review deadlines, or whether the right to recertify a corridor stays open indefinitely, is not something I know. Either answer changes what a lender should be willing to underwrite on a line crossing ethnic territory, and nobody has forced that choice yet.
