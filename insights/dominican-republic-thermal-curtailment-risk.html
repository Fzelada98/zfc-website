The Dominican Republic's curtailment problem arrived early

ZFC Partners — Insights · October 2026

Renewable penetration in the Dominican Republic rarely clears 20%, well under the roughly 40% level most power systems reach before curtailment becomes a real line item, per Global Voices' review of the grid operator's own curtailment data. In May 2025 the country curtailed 16,171 MWh of renewable output, about 18% of that month's total generation, per the Organismo Coordinador's report on the first half of 2025. The problem most markets grow into, this one walked into early.

Chile and Brazil, both markets we've written about here, curtail because renewables eventually outgrow the wire or outgrow the hour. Midday solar floods a congested corridor, or oversupplies a load that peaks somewhere else, and something has to back off. That story doesn't fit a grid still running one-fifth renewable. A different mechanism is doing the work, and it is worth naming precisely before anyone prices debt against the capacity still to come.

A thermal fleet that can't let go

The Dominican Republic's generation stack is still mostly fuel oil, coal and gas, much of it running under long-standing IPP contracts with their own minimum-load floors and must-run commitments. Those plants were built to run, not to cycle, and many carry the kind of take-or-pay structure that makes backing off expensive for someone even when backing off is exactly what the grid needs.

When solar output rises at midday, the system doesn't have a cheap way to lower that thermal floor far enough to make room. The renewable plant is the one with no contract protecting its dispatch, so it's the one asked to step aside. That is a ceiling problem turned inside out: the constraint isn't that renewables produced more than the system could ever use, it's that the firm fleet underneath them refuses to go lower.

A system can curtail its cheapest energy for the same reason at 20% renewable penetration as at 60%: because something else on the grid refuses to turn down.

That distinction matters enormously for anyone underwriting growth here. A market curtailing at high penetration gives a developer a runway, years of room before the constraint bites. A market curtailing at low penetration because of a floor, not a ceiling, offers no such runway. The next gigawatt of solar faces close to the same proportional exposure as the last one did, because the thing actually limiting dispatch hasn't moved.

The PPA was supposed to fix this

It gets worse on the revenue side. Per the state-owned distribution companies' own reporting, the average energy purchase price fell in 2025, and that drop hit the revenue of renewable PPAs whose contract price is tied to that reference benchmark rather than fixed outright. A generator holding one of these contracts is exposed twice over: once through curtailed volume it can't control, and again through a price benchmark that moves with the cost of the same thermal fleet currently squeezing it out of dispatch.

Underwriting a PPA as if the letters alone guarantee a fixed revenue line is the mistake. The honest question is what the price in that contract is actually referenced to, and whether that reference can fall for reasons that have nothing to do with how the plant performed.

What we think follows

Lenders and developers active here should model curtailment as a structural feature of today's dispatch stack, not a tail risk that only shows up once penetration climbs toward some comfortable regional average. Twenty percent already produced an 18% curtailment month. There is no obvious reason the next ten points of penetration behave differently while the thermal floor stays where it is.

To its credit, the government isn't pretending otherwise. A 600 MW renewable tender requiring bidders to pair their projects with four hours of storage had its financial bids unsealed in April 2026, per trade reporting on the process, storage attached from the start rather than added later once the market discovered it needed it, the way Chile's did. That is a real response to a real constraint, even if it arrives as a mandate rather than a market signal.

Our own pipeline sits in Peru, not the Caribbean, carrying a different version of the same discipline. Olmos Wind (~135 MW, Lambayeque, with integrated battery storage) and Frontera (~135 MW, Lambayeque) are both tracking toward Ready-to-Build in 2028, alongside Aurelion (300 MW, Moquegua, RtB 2028) and Solar II (300 MW, Moquegua, RtB 2029). Different grid, same habit: find out what is actually limiting dispatch before assuming penetration numbers tell you how much runway you have.

The open question

Four hours of paired storage shifts midday output into the evening. It is a shorter answer than the one a must-run thermal floor actually demands, if that floor is the real constraint and not just the convenient one to blame. I don't have a confident answer here, and I'd like to be wrong about the pessimistic read.

Does mandated storage actually let the thermal fleet ramp down further, or does curtailment keep showing up underneath it regardless of how many batteries get built, because the real limit is contractual, the IPP agreements written to keep those plants running, rather than technical?
