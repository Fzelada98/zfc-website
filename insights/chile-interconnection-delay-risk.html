Chile financed the panels. It didn't finance the line.

ZFC Partners — Insights · November 2026

As of May 2026, Coordinador Eléctrico Nacional was overseeing 161 transmission projects. Sixty-five of them, 40%, were running more than 10% behind their own construction schedule, per CEN's own tracking. Among new-build lines specifically, the deviation rate runs higher still, at 49%. Acera had already flagged the broader pattern in November 2025, putting more than 70% of ongoing transmission works somewhere behind plan. The 2026 numbers point to a scarcer input than capacity this year: lines arriving on the date that capacity was financed against.

Most of the Chilean grid conversation this year has gone to curtailment: solar and wind built faster than the system can absorb it, dispatched down at midday, volume risk sitting quietly inside PPAs priced on irradiance rather than settlement. That is a real problem, and it is a problem for plants that are already connected. The number above describes something that happens earlier and, for a lender, matters more: a project that finishes construction on time and then waits, mechanically complete and commercially idle, for someone else's line.

Two schedules, one borrower

A solar plant's construction path is short and comparatively controllable: twelve to eighteen months from notice to proceed to commercial operation, run by an EPC contractor the sponsor chose and can push. The transmission asset that lets that plant actually inject power runs on a different clock, built by a different developer, under a planning-to-energization cycle that Chile's National Energy Commission puts at seven to ten years in total, per CNE. The two schedules are not coupled. They are only assumed to be, in the financial model.

When the generation side hits its date and the connection side does not, the plant is finished and unpaid at the same time. Interest accrues on the construction loan, the PPA's long-stop date starts ticking, and the offtaker's patience is governed by a contract that was drafted around the sponsor's own construction risk, not a transmission concessionaire's. None of that shows up in a standard debt-sizing memo built around the generation EPC schedule and a P50 resource case.

Half of Chile's new transmission lines are now running behind their own schedule, and a well-run solar EPC contract was never going to fix that.

Why this is a harder risk than curtailment

Curtailment, once a plant is connected, is at least a known quantity you can model, hedge, or split with an offtaker. A delayed interconnection is closer to zero revenue for an unknown number of months, with no dispatch data to run a sensitivity on until the line actually energizes. It is also outside the sponsor's control in a way generation construction risk is not. You can replace an underperforming EPC contractor. You cannot replace Chile's transmission planning queue.

There is a fair counterpoint here. CEN's own US$500 million grid auction this year, and the live debate inside Chile's electricity-law reform over whether the network's economic evaluation methodology is itself holding back transmission investment, are both direct attempts to shorten this cycle. A 2028 COD project may not face the same backlog these 2026 figures describe. But a reform in debate is not a reform in force, and the projects financing today are the ones that will find out which way it goes.

What we think follows

Interconnection timing deserves its own line in the risk model, separate from generation construction risk and underwritten against the actual delivery record of the specific transmission asset or concessionaire involved, not the generation EPC contractor's track record. A bridge facility sized to the interconnection agreement's stated date, rather than to a realistic delay distribution drawn from CEN's own project data, is sized against the number the system has shown it frequently misses. Projects that connect behind an existing substation with spare injection capacity, instead of needing a new dedicated corridor, should price meaningfully tighter than greenfield ones waiting on a line that does not exist yet.

Our own development pipeline sits across the border, in southern Peru rather than Chile, but the same mechanical fact applies to any thermal-to-electron asset: the connection has to be financeable before the panels are, not after. Aurelion, our roughly 300 MW bifacial solar project in Moquegua, is still in early development, with Ready-to-Build targeted for 2028, precisely so that the interconnection work is underwritten on its own timeline rather than assumed inside the generation schedule. Fitcon Energy, the group's industrial services, EPC and O&M platform, is expanding into data-center and grid infrastructure for a related reason: a developer with its own execution capability on the connection side is less exposed to someone else's queue than one that only builds panels and waits.

The open question

Does Chile's current reform push actually compress the seven-to-ten-year cycle CNE describes, or does it mainly relocate the argument, from whether lines get built fast enough to who absorbs the cost of building them faster? I don't know which way that resolves, and I don't think anyone quoting this year's auction results confidently does either.
