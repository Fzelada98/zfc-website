Colombia's firm-energy math wasn't built for a battery

ZFC Partners — Insights · November 2026

Colombia's firm energy supply will fall 7,207 GWh-year short of demand in 2026-2027, per XM's Boletín Energético #359, a gap that widens from -2.3% of demand this year to -6.8% by 2030. The country's answer is to auction batteries into the system for the first time. The reliability-charge methodology those batteries get paid under, and the dispatch rulebook that lets an independent battery operate commercially at all, are both still being finished.

Only 1,027 MW of the 4,475 MW of new capacity expected to arrive in 2026 had come online by September 10, per XM, roughly 23% of plan. Against that backdrop, Colombia ran two auctions that, for the first time, let storage compete for long-term contracted revenue. One, run by the Bolsa Mercantil de Colombia, awarded 995 MW of solar and 100 MW of battery storage. The other, the Obligación de Energía Firme auction XM administers under CREG Resolution 101079 of 2025, awarded 1,546.9 MW of solar for 2029-2030. Capital is moving toward storage faster than the rules that price it.

What the reliability charge actually rewards

Colombia's cargo por confiabilidad pays generators for their ENFICC, firm energy for the reliability charge, a statistical estimate of how much output a plant can be counted on for during a stress year. It was built for hydro and thermal, where the variable is hydrology or fuel availability. A battery does not generate anything. It shifts energy that someone else generated into the hours the system is short. Those are different services, and crediting them with the same yardstick understates what storage is actually worth to a system running a structural deficit.

EPM has already said as much in public, asking CREG for annual solar-plus-battery auctions because the existing firm-energy framework is not closing the gap fast enough. A prior standalone battery auction inside the reliability charge did not clear the way regulators hoped, and the government has since asked CREG for an urgent reconfiguration rather than wait for the next scheduled cycle.

A reliability-charge contract prices what a model says a battery is worth. It does not yet price what the battery actually does to the hours it is dispatched into.

The part that is not finished yet

Separately from how storage gets paid, there is the question of how it operates. CREG's draft rules for batteries of 5 MW and above set out the technical, commercial and tariff conditions for independent BESS, and XM has up to twelve months from that resolution to adjust the dispatch and settlement procedures that let a standalone battery trade on its own account rather than as an appendage to a generator. Until that work is done, an independent battery's revenue stack is a reliability-charge contract sized against a methodology under active revision, plus a merchant tail in a settlement regime that does not yet formally exist for a battery operating alone.

That is not a reason to stay out. It is a reason to size debt against the piece of the revenue stack that is contracted and finished, and treat everything downstream of the pending dispatch rules as upside rather than base case. A lender underwriting a Colombian BESS project today is underwriting construction risk and regulatory-design risk in the same facility, on two different clocks.

What we think follows

The deficit numbers argue for moving fast. The rulebook argues for moving carefully. The honest response is to split the asset's cash flow into what is contracted now, the ENFICC payment under whatever methodology clears next, and what is still being negotiated in Bogotá, the actual value of shifting a megawatt-hour into the hour the grid needs it. Treating the second as collected revenue before CREG finishes the rule that defines it is the mistake Chile made pricing curtailment as a rounding error. It gets fixed eventually. The projects financed before the fix absorb the gap.

There is a fair counter. Colombia moved faster than most of the region to open any channel for storage to earn contracted revenue, and an imperfect, live methodology beats a theoretically correct one still in committee. That's worth conceding. It doesn't change what belongs in a term sheet today.

Our own pipeline sits in Peru, not Colombia, but we underwrite the identical question there in reverse. Olmos Wind, at roughly 135 MW in Lambayeque and the most advanced project in the ZFC Energy Group portfolio, carries battery storage integrated into the design from the outset rather than bolted on after Ready-to-Build, which is targeted for the first quarter of 2028. The open question for that asset is the same one Colombia is now writing rules for: whether the capacity-payment mechanism that eventually prices it credits storage for what it does, or for what an older model assumes a generator does.

The open question

XM has up to a year to finish the dispatch and settlement rules for independent batteries, and CREG has been asked to reconfigure the reliability-charge treatment of storage on an accelerated timeline. We don't know which lands first, or whether either lands calibrated to what a battery actually contributes rather than to the hydro-and-thermal logic the whole mechanism was built around. That is the number every BESS developer bidding into Colombia right now is implicitly guessing at.
