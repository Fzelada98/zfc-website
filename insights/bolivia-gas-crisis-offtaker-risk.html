Bolivia's new energy law doesn't fix its dollar problem

ZFC Partners — Insights · October 2026

Gas-fired plants supply roughly 70% of Bolivia's electricity. YPFB, the state gas company, says its own output has fallen from 59 million cubic metres a day in 2014 to 37 today, a decade without exploration investment behind it. A Harvard Growth Lab working paper puts the decline further out, 54% below the 2014 peak. Either way, the backup is diesel, and Bolivia imports about 86% of the diesel it burns, at a subsidy gap YPFB itself describes as running near $84 million a month.

That is the backdrop against which Bolivia's energy ministry put out a draft law this year to open generation, transmission and distribution to private capital. Everyone reading it in development circles is reading it as a renewables story. It is a liquidity story first, and the two get confused at exactly the point where a lender needs them separated.

A reform that reads like Peru's, a decade late

The draft Electricity and Renewable Energy Law keeps ENDE as system operator but builds a competitive framework around it: private participation in generation and distribution, a new independent regulator, and public auctions as the price-formation mechanism, according to reporting on the text published in May. On paper, that is the same toolkit Peru, Chile and Brazil already run, arriving in Bolivia roughly fifteen years after everyone else adopted it.

The optimistic read is straightforward: scarcity finally forces the reform nobody wanted to pass while gas revenue was still comfortable. A credible regulator, a real auction calendar, long-dated contracts, legal guarantees. If it holds, Bolivia gets an entry point for private solar and wind that didn't meaningfully exist before.

An auction sets a price. It doesn't create dollars.

Here is what the law doesn't touch. Bolivia has run electricity and fuel prices below cost for years, a policy the Growth Lab paper flags specifically for discouraging renewable investment, because it suppresses the price signal a developer would otherwise underwrite against. The diesel market shows the same habit in miniature: a decree this year held the price at Bs9.80 a litre for most buyers while setting a Bs18 reference rate for large consumers, a two-tier structure layered onto a subsidy the state was already struggling to fund.

An auction can clear a price in bolivianos. It cannot manufacture the dollars a contract denominated against anything harder than that actually needs.

A PPA signed under the new framework still gets paid, eventually, by the same balance sheet that right now can't source roughly a billion dollars to stabilize fuel imports, officials themselves have pointed at falling gas export revenue and the government's own fuel-import spending as the cause. Auction design and counterparty credit are separate problems. Bolivia is legislating the first while the second sits open and getting worse underneath it.

What we think follows

Price a Bolivian PPA, if one ever reaches a term sheet, off ENDE's and the Treasury's actual dollar liquidity and their payment record on existing obligations, the diesel subsidy gap being the most visible proxy available today, not off the auction clearing price or the text of a law that hasn't passed. That is the same discipline that applies anywhere a state-linked offtaker is fiscally stressed. Bolivia's version is just more acute, because the strain already shows up in keeping existing thermal plants fueled, not in a one-off missed transfer to a trust fund.

Our own pipeline sits in Peru, not Bolivia, where an independent system operator sets dispatch and a separate regulator sets tariffs, not the same state complex that also owns the upstream gas shortfall. ZFC Energy Group's 870+ MW pipeline, targeting 2 GW+ across the Americas and Europe by 2030, is underwritten against that separation. It is a different starting point than the one Bolivia's draft law is trying to build from scratch.

The open question

Does the final law pair its private auctions with a tariff reform that actually lets ENDE collect revenue covering its real costs, the part that would make a PPA payable rather than just biddable? Or does Bolivia pass an auction law while leaving the underlying price-signal and dollar problem untouched, producing contracts that look like Peru's or Chile's on paper and can't be financed the same way in practice? The law hadn't passed as of this writing, and I don't think anyone, in Bolivia or outside it, currently knows which way that goes.
