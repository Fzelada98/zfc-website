Chile's battery queue is thirteen times its battery fleet

ZFC Partners — Insights · October 2026

More than 30 GW and 114,000 MWh of battery storage is sitting in Chile's interconnection queue under system impact review, per BNamericas reporting on CEN's own tracking. Chile had roughly 2,291 MW actually operating by mid-2026, per CNE project data cited by Energy Storage News, with another 4,705 MW under construction. The queue for one technology is nearly as large as the entire Chilean grid, which stood at about 38.2 GW total installed capacity as of March 2026, per a Ministry of Energy report.

Thirteen times the operating fleet, six times the fleet under construction. Those are not rounding errors, and they are not evidence of a boom either. They are evidence that filing for a grid study in Chile costs a developer very little, which means the number tells you almost nothing about what actually gets built.

A queue position is not a project

BNamericas was careful about this, and worth being careful about too: a connection request is an early technical step, a way to ask the system operator whether there is room on a given piece of network, not a permit, not a financial close, not even site control in every case. Filing is cheap. The engineering study that follows is not, and CEN has to run one for every filing whether or not the sponsor behind it ever reaches a financing committee.

That asymmetry is the mechanism. A speculative filer loses little by reserving a queue slot early and deciding later whether to proceed. A developer who is actually trying to finance a BESS asset loses real time, because system impact studies in a congested queue are frequently sequential or cumulative, meaning the available-capacity math for a serious project further back can depend on capacity nominally claimed by filings that never convert. Every system that has let this run long enough, CAISO, MISO, parts of PJM, ended up building a cost-at-risk deposit or a cluster-study process specifically to force speculative capacity out before it clogs the pipe for everyone behind it.

What it does to a financing timeline

Lenders sizing debt against a BESS project in Chile right now cannot treat a queue position, or even a completed system impact study, as a hard date. The study's assumptions get revised as the mix of live and dead filings ahead of a given project shifts, and nobody outside CEN has full visibility into which filings are real. A developer can hold a complete environmental permit, a signed offtake structure and committed equity, and still watch its interconnection milestone move because of filings with none of those three things attached.

A queue number that runs thirteen times the operating fleet sits closer to noise than to a pipeline, and a lender who prices it as the latter is pricing the wrong signal.

We would treat the CEN queue the way a credit analyst treats an unverified reference: informative about scale, close to useless for timing. The milestone worth underwriting is the point a system impact study closes with a named, costed network upgrade attached to it, not the date a filing entered review. Two projects with identical queue numbers today can be years apart in reality, and the queue total alone will not tell you which is which.

Our own storage design work, including the integrated BESS at Olmos Wind in Lambayeque, sits in Peru rather than Chile's SEN, so we are not exposed to this specific queue. The discipline carries across borders regardless: a battery's interconnection date belongs in a project's design and financing plan from day one, treated as a variable to manage rather than a line copied out of a government tracker.

What we think follows

Chile's regulators have an obvious lever here that other systems have already pulled: attach a real financial deposit, scaled to project size and forfeited on withdrawal or missed milestones, to a connection request. That would not eliminate speculation, sponsors routinely treat deposits as a cost of optionality worth paying, but it would shrink the queue to something closer to the capacity actually being pursued, and it would shorten study timelines for the projects behind it.

There is a fair counterargument against moving fast on that. A deposit requirement set too high locks out smaller developers and new entrants who cannot post capital before they have a study telling them the site even works, which is exactly the chicken-and-egg problem Chile's current light-touch process was presumably built to avoid. Getting the number right matters more than getting it done quickly.

The open question

I don't know whether Chile moves on this before the queue grows large enough to force the question, the way congestion forced it in parts of the US market, or whether the storage boom simply outruns the problem because enough real projects get built that the speculative share stops mattering in practice. Both paths are plausible from where the data sits today, and I'd genuinely like to be told which one the regulator is actually watching for.
