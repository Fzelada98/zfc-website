Peru's regional compute pitch is ahead of its own grid

ZFC Partners — Insights · November 2026

On November 18, Peru's government floated a regional alliance with Colombia, Ecuador and Chile to offer shared compute capacity along the Pacific coast, holding up Brazil's REDATA framework as the model worth copying, per Telesemana's reporting. Six months earlier, on May 2, COES had already placed the Centro-Norte transmission corridor under Article 95's "Situación Excepcional," an emergency dispatch mechanism that lets the system operator exceed the line's normal transfer limits, per a resolution published in El Peruano. One of those facts is a pitch. The other is an operating constraint. Peru is advertising headroom it has not yet shown it has.

Most of the coverage of Peru's AI ambitions runs through the first fact. Fewer people are reading the COES resolutions, and almost nobody is holding the two side by side.

What the alliance pitch skips

Peru's actual data-center base today is enterprise colocation serving the local market, a different animal from the training campuses this pitch imagines. GTD, the Chilean operator backed by Grupo Romero, is putting roughly $13 million into a second phase of its Lurín facility, adding about 20 MW across roughly 960 racks, per ProActivo and DatacenterDynamics. Artizon's own estimate, cited by Gestión, puts Peru's entire data-center market at close to $378 million by 2031. Set that against the gigawatt-scale queues now being fought over in Querétaro, São Paulo and Santiago, and the distance between "Peru wants a seat at the regional compute table" and what is actually under construction carries most of the argument this piece is making.

That gap would matter less if Peru's grid had obvious slack to grow into. It doesn't, at least not everywhere, and the place it doesn't is instructive.

What "Situación Excepcional" tells a lender, not a press release

Article 95 exists for exactly the situation COES invoked it for: demand on a corridor, driven by mining and industrial load growth in the north, outrunning the transmission capacity built to carry it, with no near-term reinforcement ready to take the strain instead. Running a link past its ordinary limits is a workable short-term fix, and it doubles as a disclosure to anyone doing diligence: the margin on that corridor is already spent, before a single rack of AI infrastructure has asked for a connection.

Running a corridor past its own limits is the plainest signal COES will give anyone this year: the system's spare capacity is already gone, before new load of any kind shows up.

For a generator sitting behind a constrained node, that margin shows up as basis risk: the nodal price it actually realizes can diverge from the system price a PPA was priced against, and congestion cost allocation on an exceptional-regime line is not the same exercise as pricing it on a healthy one. A hyperscaler's site-selection team runs a cruder version of the same check. It wants to see margin before it wants to see ambition.

What we think follows

MINEM is pushing 32 transmission projects across 20 regions, with $2.109 billion in offered investment aimed at exactly this kind of congestion, per the ministry's own figures. That pipeline, not the alliance announcement, is the precondition. A regional compute-capacity pitch is cheap to make and costs nothing to walk back. A transmission reinforcement takes years and a functioning concession process, which is its own well-documented chokepoint in Peru. Until some of that $2.1 billion shows up as energized capacity, the alliance is a position paper standing in front of a procurement queue.

There is a real counterargument, and it deserves airtime. The November proposal may be mostly about fiber routing, not generation: Peru already sits on the SPSC/Mistral subsea cable linking Ecuador, Peru and Chile, and a regional compute play built on connectivity rather than new power-hungry campuses would make the grid argument above largely beside the point. That is plausible, and it is also incomplete. Whatever anchors a "regional compute node," in Lima, Lurín or anywhere else on this grid, still draws real megawatts from a real corridor, and the corridor question does not go away just because the pitch is framed around cables instead of power plants.

ZFC's own position in Peru sits away from the specific corridor under strain. Aurelion, our roughly 300 MW bifacial solar project in Moquegua, in southern Peru, is in early development with Ready-to-Build targeted for 2028, on a different part of the system than the Centro-Norte link. Fitcon Energy, our industrial services, EPC and O&M platform, is expanding into data-center and grid infrastructure. We operate as long-term owners, which is the posture this kind of sequencing problem rewards: it takes years to close, not quarters.

The open question

Does MINEM's $2.1 billion transmission pipeline convert into measurable, energized headroom before any version of this alliance moves past a press release, or does Peru keep announcing a regional role that the grid underneath it has not yet earned the right to claim? I do not know which way that resolves, and I have not seen anyone in COES or MINEM commit to a timeline that would settle it.
