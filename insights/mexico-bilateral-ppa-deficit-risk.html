Mexico fixed who controls the grid. It didn't fix what a lender can size debt against.

ZFC Partners — Insights · November 2026

Mexico's 2025 electricity reform settled the fight over control. CFE keeps at least 54% of everything injected into the national grid, full stop. What it didn't settle is what a lender now underwrites a renewable project against, because the instrument that used to anchor that debt, a 20-year auction contract with a quasi-sovereign buyer, is no longer the default route to market. Selected private projects sell power through bilateral contracts with industrial offtakers or into the wholesale market instead. That shift arrived at almost the same moment IMCO put Mexico's generation shortfall above 48,000 GWh by 2030 if transmission investment slips on schedule.

Most coverage of the reform stops at the ownership question, because that was the loud fight and it produced a clean number. The quieter change is what happens on the page a credit committee actually reads.

The contract that disappeared

A CFE auction PPA, the product of four completed long-term rounds run between 2016 and 2017, gave lenders something specific: a fixed tariff, a 20-year tenor built to match debt amortization, and a buyer whose payment history and termination liability had already been tested across a real portfolio of deals. It wasn't perfect. It was known.

The 2025 framework doesn't reinstate that mechanism as the default. Procurement now runs through mixed CFE-private tenders, a new self-consumption fast-track, and direct bilateral contracts between a generator and an end consumer. Long-term auctions are no longer mandatory for CFE's own supply. A developer closing a deal with a steel plant, an auto manufacturer, or a hyperscale data center operator is signing a contract with one company, not with the state.

What that does to a DSCR

A CFE auction contract was quasi-sovereign credit risk, underwritten once and then relied on across a whole asset class. A bilateral deal with a single industrial offtaker is a corporate loan wearing a renewable project's clothes, and it comes with none of that inherited history.

A bilateral PPA signed with one industrial offtaker is a single-name corporate credit, not a utility contract, and the market doesn't yet price it that way.

Tenor is the first mismatch. Debt on a Mexican renewable asset still wants 18 to 20 years. An early industrial offtaker, still feeling its way through a market that didn't require this structure a year ago, is unlikely to sign that long, which leaves a merchant tail with no settlement history behind it. Termination language is the second. Four completed auction rounds built a body of precedent around what happens when a CFE contract breaks. Nothing comparable exists yet for a bilateral deal, because almost none have been tested through a default in a Mexican court.

The deficit makes it worse, not better

None of this would matter as much in a market with slack to spare. Mexico doesn't have that slack. IMCO's July 2026 analysis of Plan México's own growth targets puts 2030 electricity demand at 454,035 GWh, 10.1% above what the official PLADESE plan assumes, which implies roughly 11.9 GW of generation capacity beyond what's already in the pipeline. Layer that on top of IMCO's separate 48,000 GWh shortfall warning and the picture is a grid that may ration regardless of who signed the contract.

A shortage doesn't curtail politely in credit order. When CENACE has to dispatch around a constraint, the plant gets backed down whether its offtaker is a one-notch-above-junk manufacturer or a flagship hyperscaler. Credit risk on the contract and delivery risk on the grid stack on top of each other. They don't substitute for one another the way a well-structured market might let them.

What we think follows

Lenders should price a bilateral Mexican PPA closer to a bespoke corporate loan, covenant package, parent guarantee if one exists, security over the offtaker's own receivables, than to the auction paper it replaced. Developers chasing the willing early movers in steel, automotive, and data centers should expect a credit spread for that novelty until a real base of financed, tested contracts exists.

There's a fair counterargument. CFE's own 46% cap on private injection and its new Strategic Projects window give the state a direct interest in seeing these bilateral deals succeed, since it still needs the capacity whoever signs for it. That alignment is real, and it's worth something. It still isn't a signed backstop, and treating it as one is the mistake worth avoiding.

Our own pipeline sits in Peru, not Mexico, where Osinergmin still runs fixed-price utility auctions even though it hasn't called a fifth round since 2024. Different mechanism, same discipline: grade the actual entity signing the contract, not the law that describes how the market is supposed to work.

The open question

Does a real body of bankable bilateral precedent, financed deals, tested termination clauses, a default or two that shows how CENACE and the courts actually behave, build fast enough to catch up with Mexico's own deficit numbers before 2030? Or does the industrial electrification plan outrun both the grid and the paperwork meant to finance it, at the same time?
