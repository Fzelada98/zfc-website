Peru sells clean power by the year. Hyperscalers buy it by the hour

ZFC Partners — Insights · November 2026

Wind and solar supplied 10% of Peru's electricity in 2025, per Osinergmin, almost all of it from daytime solar with no storage mandate attached. Every pitch deck selling Peru as a clean-power destination for AI load leans on that number. It is the wrong number. The buyers Peru most wants to attract no longer measure clean supply once a year. They measure it hour by hour, and the hour-by-hour picture behind that 10% has not been shown to anyone yet.

A quiet change in what "clean" means to a buyer

For most of the last decade, a renewable claim meant an annual certificate: generate one megawatt-hour somewhere on the grid over a year, consume one elsewhere, call it matched. I-RECs, the certificate standard that dominates Latin America, work exactly this way. They say nothing about which hour the clean electron and the dirty electron changed places.

Google and Microsoft have both set 2030 targets for 24/7 carbon-free energy, matching consumption against clean generation hour by hour rather than annually, and the UN-backed 24/7 CFE Compact now counts more than 100 signatories, per WRI. EnergyTag's Granular Certificate Scheme Standard exists specifically because an annual I-REC cannot prove what happened at 2am. None of that machinery has reached Peru's market yet. Brazil is further along and still arguing about whether it should bother, per S&P Global reporting on the debate inside the GHG Protocol revision.

An annual certificate proves you generated enough clean power somewhere. An hourly one proves you had it when you needed it. Those are different products, and Peru is still selling the first one.

Why one solar PPA cannot do what a hyperscaler asks for

A single-technology solar contract covers the hours the sun is out and nothing else. Call it six to seven strong hours a day in southern Peru's irradiance. Against an annual I-REC obligation, that is a complete answer: generate the MWh, match the MWh, done. Against an hourly CFE target, it is a partial one, and the uncovered two-thirds of the day does not disappear. It gets filled by whatever the grid is running at 9pm, which in Peru today is mostly thermal and hydro. That is precisely why AES's 24/7 deal with Google and Microsoft in Virginia leans on a diversified resource mix rather than a single solar asset, per WRI's reporting on the arrangement. A buyer chasing a real hourly match needs a basket: solar for the day, something else for the night, and a contract structure that prices the shape, not just the volume. SEIN's generation mix as of mid-2026 still runs through 14,492 MW of installed capacity led by thermal at 7,032 MW and hydro at 5,343 MW, with solar and wind supplying the smaller, intermittent slice. That mix can firm a hyperscaler's load. It has not yet been contracted to.

What this does to the pitch, and to the deal

The colocation and mid-market compute Peru is actually landing today, at scale like GTD's Lurín build-out, does not usually carry a contractual 24/7 CFE requirement. That demand is real and bankable on today's PPA structures. The hyperscaler-grade demand, the kind that writes a load letter in the hundreds of megawatts, is a different buyer with a different ask, and it is not going to accept an I-REC and a smile. If Peru's developers keep offering the same single-technology daytime product to both buyers, the mid-market deal closes and the hyperscaler one quietly goes to whichever market already has a firmed, shaped, hourly-matchable offer on the table.

There is a fair counterpoint. Not every hyperscaler enforces hourly matching as a hard gate today, and the GHG Protocol debate in Brazil shows informed people disagree about whether hourly matching should even be the global bar. A developer betting the strategy on a 2030 standard that might soften is taking on a different risk than one ignoring it outright.

ZFC's own development pipeline is not immune to this critique. Aurelion, our roughly 300 MW bifacial solar project in early development in Moquegua, with Ready-to-Build targeted for 2028, is exactly the single-technology daytime asset this piece describes. It has no storage component today and is not contracted to, or designed for, any data-center load. Fitcon Energy, our industrial services, EPC and O&M platform, is expanding into data-center and grid infrastructure work, which is a different and narrower claim than owning the generation stack a hyperscaler would need. We hold assets as a long-term owner, which at least means we will be the ones living with whatever procurement standard wins this argument.

The open question

Peru can build the firming and the certificate infrastructure that hourly matching requires, or it can keep selling annual clean MWh to buyers who have already moved past counting them that way. Nobody in the market has committed to the first path yet, including us. Does the hourly-matching standard actually harden into the global requirement hyperscalers are signaling, or does it stay a frontier-market concern while Peru keeps winning the mid-tier load that still runs on an I-REC? That is an open question, and the honest answer right now is that nobody has it.
